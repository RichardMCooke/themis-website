02 What exit readiness actually involves Buyers don't pay for potential. They pay for evidence — and they start collecting it long before you do.

The three things that move the price

01

The eighteen-month runway

  • Months 18–12. Fix what a buyer would discount: margin leakage, loss-making lines, one-offs sitting in run-rate.
  • Months 12–6. Two clean filed years, a monthly pack that ties to them, and a forecast you can defend line by line.
  • Months 6–0. Data room, advisers, tax structuring — and a team that can run it while you sell it.
  • Work back from your filing dates. A weak year inside the last two costs more than a strong year gains.

Why it matters

EBITDA is bought on trailing performance. Improvements made after the process starts land in the buyer’s numbers, not your price.

02

Diligence hygiene

  • Reconciled to the pound. Stock, WIP, accruals, intercompany, director loans — no unexplained residuals.
  • Contracts signed and on file. Customers, suppliers, leases, IP, employment, and anything with a change-of-control clause.
  • Statutory house in order. Filings current, share register clean, options and consents documented.
  • One version of the numbers. Management accounts that bridge to the accounts, with the bridge written down.

Why it matters

Every question you can’t answer quickly becomes a price chip, an indemnity, or money held back in retention.

03

The equity story

  • What is actually being bought. Recurring revenue, defensible margin, and headroom the buyer can see.
  • A three-year plan already part-delivered. Year one substantially banked before anyone reads it.
  • Growth the buyer can execute. Geography, capacity, cross-sell — not growth that depends on the founder.
  • Normalised EBITDA that survives challenge. Add-backs evidenced, not asserted.

Why it matters

Price is arithmetic. The multiple is a judgement about risk and future — and the story is what moves it.

What else gets tested

Customer concentration

Top five as a share of revenue — and the contractual basis sitting under it.

Owner dependency

What breaks in the week after completion if you stop answering the phone.

Working capital

The normal level, agreed before the SPA — not argued about afterwards.

Management depth

A second line that stays, and is incentivised to stay through earn-out.

What nobody says out loud

Readiness isn’t a document.

It’s the condition of the business. The data room is the last five per cent — the other ninety-five is trading history you can no longer change.

The price is largely set before the process starts.

By the time the information memorandum is written, the numbers it describes have already happened.

Most founders start too late.

Twelve weeks isn’t preparation — it’s tidying. The value is created in the eighteen months nobody wants to spend.

Get ready eighteen months early, or negotiate the difference later.

03 Board reporting a buyer will trust Diligence doesn't begin at the data room. It begins with twenty-four months of board packs.

The pack, the challenge, the gaps

01

The monthly pack

  • One summary page first. Revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, cash, net debt and order book — actual, budget and prior year.
  • P&L, balance sheet and cash flow together. A P&L alone says nothing about working capital.
  • Ten working days, every month. Timeliness is itself evidence of control.
  • Written commentary, owned by a named person. Variances explained — not a spreadsheet dump.
  • A KPI page. The six operational numbers that drive the financial ones.

Why it matters

A buyer reads two years of packs in sequence. Consistency of format is being tested as hard as the content.

02

The numbers that get challenged

  • Gross margin by customer, product and site — not one blended percentage.
  • Normalised EBITDA and every add-back, with the evidence sitting beside it.
  • Stock and WIP. Valuation method, provision policy, ageing profile.
  • Revenue recognition and cut-off, particularly on contract and project work.
  • Accruals, deferred income and rebates — anything resting on a judgement.
  • Cash conversion. EBITDA to cash, explained rather than assumed.

Why it matters

Diligence tests whether your numbers behave the same way when somebody else cuts them.

03

Where the gaps usually are

  • Management accounts that don’t bridge to the filed accounts, with no reconciliation on file.
  • A balance sheet reviewed annually, reconciliations left to the auditor to find.
  • No budget — or one abandoned by month four.
  • A forecast that is a re-typed budget, rather than a rolling view of what will actually happen.
  • Numbers that change after issue with no record of why or by whom.
  • KPIs that measure activity, not money.

Why it matters

Every one of these is fixable in a quarter. None of them are fixable once diligence has started.

AI and judgement — getting the balance right

What the tools do well

Use them, and use them properly

  • Reconciliation, coding and matching at volume, without fatigue or drift
  • Anomaly detection across the ledger — duplicates, outliers, missing postings
  • Drafting first-pass variance commentary straight from the data
  • Consolidation, data preparation and building the pack itself
  • Answering “what is inside this number” in seconds rather than days

What still needs a qualified finance mind

This is what a buyer is buying

  • Deciding what is material and what is noise
  • Judgement calls — provisions, valuation, recognition, add-backs
  • Knowing which variance the board actually needs to act on
  • Owning the number in front of a buyer, an auditor or a lender
  • The “should we actually do this deal” call

Automate the preparation. Never automate the judgement. A buyer will ask who signed the number — and “the model” is not an answer.

If the pack doesn’t convince your board, it will not convince a buyer.

04 Succession in a family business The hardest questions are rarely the financial ones — but they all arrive in the numbers eventually.

The sequence — and the order matters

01

Governance first

  • A board that actually meets. A date, a pack, a decision, minutes. The kitchen table is not governance.
  • Separate the three tables. Family, board and management — same people, different hats, different meetings.
  • A family charter. How decisions get made, how disputes get settled, how a family member joins the business.
  • One voice from outside the family. Someone at the table with no inheritance at stake.
  • Dividend policy written down before the year it becomes contentious.

Why it matters

Governance is the only one of the three you can build while everyone still agrees. It is what carries the family through the point where they don’t.

02

Roles second

  • Real jobs with real accountabilities — applied to family and non-family alike.
  • Market rate for the role performed, not for the surname.
  • An honest answer on whether they want it. Asked properly, asked privately, and believed.
  • Experience earned outside before a senior seat inside.
  • A route for capable non-family managers — or you lose them at precisely the wrong moment.
  • The founder’s role defined by a date, not by feel.

Why it matters

Most succession failures are not about who owns the shares. They are about someone holding a job they could not do, or never wanted.

03

Ownership third

  • A structure that separates economic value from control where those two things need to sit apart.
  • A shareholders’ agreement. Pre-emption, valuation method, deadlock, and a way out for a family member who wants one.
  • Wills, trusts and reliefs reviewed together, not by three advisers separately — and kept current as the rules move.
  • A valuation everyone has seen, on a method everyone has agreed, before anyone needs it.
  • Fairness for those not in the business — settled deliberately, not left to a will.
  • Test it against a death, a divorce and a falling-out, not just a retirement.

Why it matters

Ownership decisions are slow to reverse and expensive to get wrong. Take them once governance and roles are settled, and they get far easier.

Staying involved after the handover

Where it works

Defined, visible, time-bound

  • A defined role with a defined end date, in writing, seen by the management team
  • Chairman or non-executive — contributing at the board, not shadowing the MD
  • Customer, supplier and technical relationships handed over deliberately, one by one
  • Any earn-out tied to things the seat you hold can still influence
  • A successor you back in public, including when you disagree in private

Where it goes wrong

The failure modes we see most

  • “I’ll stay involved” — with no shared definition of involved
  • Staff who keep walking past the new MD’s office to reach yours
  • An earn-out resting on decisions somebody else now makes
  • A retained minority stake with no mechanism to realise it
  • No answer to what you do on the Monday after

The question is not whether you stay involved. It is whether everyone can describe what involved means — in one sentence, and the same sentence.

Governance, then roles, then ownership. Any other order costs more.

05 Death by data: don't produce your Monday report this week Being data-driven is not the same as producing data for data's sake.

Some businesses have confused being “data-driven” with producing data for data's sake — more reports, more dashboards, more spreadsheets analysing other spreadsheets.

The three questions before Monday

01

What you produce

List every recurring report. Who reads it, and how long after the period it lands.

Why it matters

A buyer, and your own board, judge you on signal — not on volume.

02

What decision it enables

For each report, name the decision that couldn't be made without it.

Why it matters

If the answer is none, the report is a habit, not a control.

03

What happens if you stop

Skip it once. See how long it takes anyone to notice, or ask for it back.

Why it matters

Silence is the fastest audit of what your reporting actually does.

Data-rich or decision-poor?

Signs you're drowning in data

Volume, not signal

  • A management pack running to 40 pages, most of it unread.
  • KPIs that measure activity, not money.
  • Numbers compiled, checked and circulated after they’re already stale.
  • Sales, Ops and Finance each learning about their own numbers from a report, not from knowing.

What good looks like

Fewer numbers, better decisions

  • One dashboard: cash, risk, what changed, what happens next.
  • Departments that know their own numbers before month-end confirms them.
  • Real-time and automated where it can be, judged where it must be.
  • Every report traceable to a decision it exists to support.

Month-end should confirm what good leaders already know. It shouldn't reveal it.

06 The bottleneck test: two weeks away If the business genuinely can't function for a fortnight without you, that is a structural problem — and a discount on the valuation.

“If your business genuinely can't function for a fortnight without you, then you've failed as a leader.”

On returning from two weeks away, and what it revealed

The four questions to ask yourself

01

Is it because they aren’t capable?

Or because you’ve never given them the autonomy to be capable — have you invested enough time in developing them, or just found it quicker to decide everything yourself?

02

What are you actually adding?

Every decision routed through you can feel like value. In reality, it often just means you’ve become the bottleneck the whole business waits on.

03

What would break if you didn’t answer your phone?

If the honest answer is “nothing, really”, that’s a sign of strength. If it’s “everything”, that’s a structural problem, not a compliment.

04

Who would make the call in your absence?

Not who would ring you first — who would actually decide, sign it off, and live with the outcome without needing your say-so.

What a buyer does with this

A buyer prices key-person risk directly into the multiple they're willing to pay. A business that can't run without its owner is, to them, a riskier asset — and every year of that dependency is a discount on the valuation, not a neutral fact about how it's run. Reducing that reliance is not a soft management goal; it is one of the most direct ways a leader adds real, measurable value to the equity they're building.

What good delegation looks like

  • A leadership team that keeps the business moving while one person is away, regardless of seniority.
  • Capable people equipped with the skills to make decisions, and the confidence to make them without approval.
  • A short, genuinely exceptional list of things that warrant a call — not a standing expectation of one.
  • A team trusted with real decisions long before the holiday, not just handed the phone number.
  • A leader whose absence tests the business, rather than exposes it.

The less a business depends on you day to day, the more successful you've been as a leader.

07 “We can't do AI until the data's sorted” The foundations project has a start date, a budget and a steering committee. What it doesn't have is a finish date.

It's reasonable advice — the master data's a mess, the ERP doesn't talk to the planning tool, three systems hold a version of the customer that none of them agree on. But the foundations project has a start date, a budget and a steering committee. What it doesn't have, and never has, is a finish date.

Where the blocker actually moves to

You can export a file today, point a model at it with the right instructions and proper checks, and it will do work that used to take a week — no integration project required. But a few weeks in, the constraint stops being the data and becomes the person reading the output. Who on the team can look at a plausible, beautifully formatted answer and spot the assumption that's quietly wrong? Who's curious enough to keep taking the process apart rather than accepting the first draft? That's the real audit — and it can't happen while everyone is still waiting for the systems project to land.

What this means in practice

01

Pick one process this month

Choose something that doesn't need a system change to try — board commentary, a reconciliation, a first-draft forecast. Export, upload, run it properly, then compare the result to what a person produced. One process, four weeks, a real answer either way.

02

Write the checks before the prompt

Traceable formulas and a documented source for every assumption, before you write a word to the model. A marketing team can live with an odd hallucination in a first draft; someone will ask you where a number came from eleven months after you produced it, and the answer needs to exist.

03

Keep the systems roadmap running

Same pace, same budget, no pause. This is what you do while the foundations are being laid, not instead of doing it — the teams that get this wrong pick one and quietly drop the other.

04

Watch who takes to it

The person who rebuilds the output twice because the first version wasn't good enough has just told you something a competency framework never would. They're your next AI-in-Finance champion — and possibly the case for keeping someone else exactly where they are.

The data will never be finished — that's just what data looks like in a growing business, with another acquisition, another product line, another system somebody bought without asking. The better question is whether you're willing to work in the house while it's being fixed, and whether the people in there with you are the ones you'd want alongside you when it's done.

The systems project will take eighteen months. One exported file can start on Tuesday.

08 Your boardroom doesn't need an accountant. It needs a finance leader. Being qualified to account for a business and qualified to help lead one are not the same thing.

There is a difference — a bloody big one. Qualifications matter, but we've spent far too long confusing being qualified to account for a business with being qualified to help lead one. Immaculate management accounts and a balance sheet reconciled to the penny are admirable. They're not the same thing as driving the business forward.

The accountant

Behind the Finance door

  • Arrives at 8:30, disappears behind the Finance door.
  • Immaculate management accounts, reconciled to the penny.
  • Sales call them “the guy from Accounts”.
  • Marketing aren’t entirely convinced they’ve actually met.
  • Nobody outside Finance knows their name.

The finance leader

In the business, not beside it

  • Everywhere — talking to Sales, understanding Operations, annoying Marketing.
  • Speaks to customers, asks questions, actually understands how the business works.
  • People tell them things before decisions are made.
  • Involved before the expensive call, not explaining it afterwards.
  • Commercial instinct, relationships and influence, not just controls.

The numbers tell you what

Margin fell. Aged debt is rising. Headcount is up. The P&L, the debtor ledger and the payroll report each state a fact — reliably, and after it's already happened.

People tell you why

Credit Control knows why customers aren't paying. Operations knows why one team is drowning while another has time to spare. The spreadsheet doesn't gossip — people do, and only to leaders they know and trust.

Why this matters at the board table

People tell things to finance leaders they know and trust. That's how Finance gets involved before an expensive decision is made — not afterwards, explaining why it was expensive.

So yes, give me brilliant accountants — technical expertise, controls, compliance and accurate reporting. But at the boardroom table, give me someone with commercial instinct, curiosity, operational understanding, relationships and influence too — someone who understands the numbers and the business that creates them. Those things don't come printed on a certificate.

Being qualified to account for a business and qualified to lead one are not the same thing.

09 Tax planning for UK SMEs: timing, reliefs and the decisions that matter Almost every planning point here is a timing point. Very few reduce the total tax paid — most change when it is paid, and what it costs in cash along the way.

Technical briefing · September 2026

Good tax planning for a UK SME is rarely about clever structuring. In practice it is about getting the timing right on decisions the business would be making anyway: when to invest in equipment, when to invest in research and development, how and when directors take money out of the company, when to declare a dividend, and when to start thinking seriously about succession.

Two Budgets in the last two years, together with reforms already legislated for 2026, have moved most of the dials that owner-managed businesses rely on. Capital allowances, R&D relief, the charge on overdrawn director’s loan accounts, dividend tax rates, employee share schemes, Business Asset Disposal Relief and Business Property Relief have all changed. Several changed twice.

Why the distinction matters

Full expensing is a timing difference rather than a permanent saving — it accelerates relief that would otherwise arrive over several years, and it is clawed back on disposal. The value is in the cash flow and in the rate at which relief is obtained, not in the headline percentage. The same is true of loss relief, instalment payments and most extraction planning.

1 · Timing capital expenditure

01

The three routes to 100% relief

  • AIA — £1m a year. Companies, sole traders and most partnerships; new and second-hand assets; now a permanent feature.
  • Full expensing — uncapped. Companies only, new and unused main-pool plant and machinery. Not assets bought for leasing, not cars, not second-hand.
  • 50% FYA on new special rate expenditure.
  • Sequence the claims. Point AIA at expenditure that would otherwise attract only the special rate, since full expensing cannot reach it at 100%.

Why it matters

Run together properly, a company can get 100% on up to £1m of second-hand or special-rate spend through AIA while using uncapped full expensing for new main-pool assets.

02

Two changes that alter the calculus

  • From 1 January 2026, a new 40% first-year allowance applies to qualifying main-rate plant and machinery not covered by AIA or full expensing.
  • From 1 April 2026, the main pool writing-down allowance falls from 18% to 14%. Expenditure left in the general pool now takes roughly a quarter longer to write down.
  • Straddling periods are calculated on a blended basis, apportioned by days either side of the change.

Why it matters

The slower the pool unwinds, the more the year in which you buy is worth arguing about.

03

The disposal trap

  • Full expensing carries a clawback AIA does not. Disposal proceeds come in as an immediate balancing charge under CAA 2001 s59A, not as a reduction of the pool.
  • Claimed on the whole cost? The charge equals the whole disposal value. On part, a proportion applies. On 50% FYA assets, half the disposal value.
  • For kit you’ll sell on soon, AIA or writing-down allowances may leave the company better off.
  • The fixed asset register becomes compliance, not housekeeping — cost, date, and which allowance was claimed.

Why it matters

Front-loading relief that is then clawed back in full is not a saving. It is a loan.

Buildings

Non-residential construction, renovation and conversion falls outside the plant and machinery regime and attracts the Structures and Buildings Allowance: 3% straight line over 33⅓ years, on contracts from 29 October 2018. No first-year relief, no acceleration — the only timing decision is when the building is brought into use. An allowance statement is a statutory requirement, and a buyer of a used structure must obtain a copy from the previous owner. On any project, have the spend analysed between the structure and the plant embedded in it — heating, lighting, ventilation, sanitary ware. The plant element reaches 100%; the structure crawls at 3%. The split is often worth several times the cost of the exercise.

Vehicles

Cars are excluded from both AIA and full expensing, with one exception: new and unused zero-emission cars qualify for a 100% first-year allowance, as does expenditure on charge points — now running to 31 March 2027 for corporation tax and 5 April 2027 for income tax. Used electric cars go into the main pool at 14%. The benefit-in-kind position is correspondingly favourable: 4% for a fully electric company car in 2026/27, rising to 5%, 7% and 9% by 2029/30, against rates approaching 37% for conventional cars. A 100% deduction plus a single-figure benefit charge remains one of the more attractive reliefs available.

2 · R&D and the Patent Box

Merged scheme or ERIS?

Model it before the year end, not after

  • Merged R&D Expenditure Credit. 20% above the line, worth c.14.7p–16.2p per £1 after tax, any size of company. Loss-makers suffer notional tax at 19%, which is why the net rate lands at 16.2p.
  • ERIS. Loss-making SMEs at 30%+ R&D intensity: 86% additional deduction, loss surrendered for a 14.5% payable credit — up to c.27p per £1. A one-year grace period applies if intensity temporarily drops.
  • ERIS is not automatically better. The 27p assumes losses to surrender. Near break-even there is little to surrender, and the flat merged rate can win.
  • PAYE cap. Cash is capped at £20,000 plus 300% of relevant PAYE and NIC — biting hardest where R&D is delivered by subcontractors or low-salaried directors.
  • Contracted-out R&D can now be included regardless of who the work goes to, subject to conditions, restricted to 65% where the third party is unconnected. Often missed.
  • Overseas costs — subcontractors and externally provided workers outside the UK — have been restricted since April 2024, subject to exemptions.

Compliance timing now matters as much as the claim

Missed dates lose good claims

  • Six-month advance notification. First-time claimants, or anyone who has not claimed in three years, must notify HMRC within six months of the period end. Miss it and the claim is lost outright, regardless of merit.
  • Additional Information Form with or before the CT600, naming a senior officer and setting out projects and costs.
  • Contemporaneous documentation of the technological uncertainty being resolved. HMRC’s compliance campaign means a claim that once passed unexamined no longer does.
  • Patent Box. A 10% effective rate on profits from qualifying patents, against a 25% main rate — complementary to R&D relief, not an alternative. The nexus fraction limits the benefit to R&D you performed yourself or subcontracted to unconnected parties. Election applies from the start of the period in which it is made. For an SME that files patents and does not claim it, this is frequently the largest unclaimed relief on the balance sheet.

3 · Director’s loans — two charges, different triggers

01

Section 455 — 35.75%

Charged on a close company where a loan to a participator is still outstanding nine months and one day after the period end. The rate tracks the higher dividend rate: 33.75% to 5 April 2026, 35.75% after.

Repayable — but slowly. The claim cannot be made until nine months after the end of the period in which the repayment falls, and the money arrives nine months and one day after that period end. Claims must be made within four years. Interest charged on the s455 liability is not recoverable.

It cannot be timed around: a repayment of £5,000 or more followed by a new advance within 30 days is disregarded, and a broader arrangements rule catches repayments always intended to be redrawn. A properly accrued and paid bonus is a payment of earnings and can clear a loan account cleanly.

02

Beneficial loan interest

A separate charge arises where the balance exceeds £10,000 at any point in the tax year. Charge interest below HMRC’s official rate — 3.75% from 6 April 2025, reviewed quarterly — and the shortfall is a taxable benefit: income tax for the director, Class 1A at 15% for the company, reported on P11D.

Charging at or above the official rate removes this exposure entirely, and has no bearing at all on the section 455 position. It is perfectly possible to have both charges on the same loan account.

The most common and most expensive error here is treating the director’s loan account as a year-end problem. By the time the accounts are being prepared, the options have already narrowed.

4 · Profit extraction: dividends, bonuses, pensions

Dividends

From 6 April 2026 the basic rate rose from 8.75% to 10.75% and the higher rate from 33.75% to 35.75%. The additional rate is unchanged at 39.35% and the £500 allowance is unchanged. Personal allowance, higher rate and additional rate thresholds are frozen until April 2031, so fiscal drag keeps pushing dividend income into higher bands without any further rate change.

Why it matters

Because s455 tracks the higher dividend rate, the same measure that made dividends dearer also made leaving a loan account overdrawn dearer.

Bonuses and the nine-month rule

Remuneration accrued at a period end is deductible in that period only if actually paid within nine months (CTA 2009 s1288). Paid later, the deduction moves to the period of payment — and where the computation is prepared before the nine months expire and the amount is still unpaid, it must be added back and the return amended later if it is paid.

This catches holiday pay accruals and unallocated bonus pools, not just director bonuses. PAYE runs on the earliest of several dates, so the PAYE period and the corporation tax deduction period can easily fall in different years.

The discipline

Hold a board meeting before the year end recording the bonus, accrue it, and pay it within nine months.

Pensions

Deductible subject to wholly-and-exclusively, and outside employer NIC entirely — unlike salary or bonus. Annual allowance £60,000 for 2025/26 and 2026/27, tapered by £1 for every £2 of adjusted income above £260,000 to a £10,000 floor, with a £200,000 threshold-income gateway. Three years of unused allowance can be carried forward.

The timing rule is stricter than for most expenses and regularly missed: under FA 2004 s196 relief is available only for contributions actually paid in the period, in cleared funds. The money must physically leave the account before the year end.

Contributions above 210% of the prior period can be spread forward over up to four periods. And from 6 April 2029, NIC relief on salary-sacrificed contributions will be capped at the first £2,000 a year.

Why it matters

On a large catch-up contribution the cash leaves in one year while the relief may not. Model it before committing.

5 · Corporation tax, thresholds and payment timing

The marginal relief band

19% to £50,000, 25% above £250,000, marginal relief in between — an effective 26.5% on each additional pound inside the band, higher than the main rate itself. A company inside the band gets 26.5p in the pound from deductible spend, against 25p above it. Conversely, dropping below £50,000 saves at 19% on everything below that point.

Associated companies

The £50,000 and £250,000 limits, the instalment thresholds and the £5m loss deductions allowance are all divided by associated companies plus one. The test replaced the older 51% group test from 1 April 2023 and is broader — common control, not shareholding percentages. Dormant companies are generally excluded. One forgotten trading company can halve the thresholds for everything else.

Instalments

“Large” above £1.5m of augmented profits, “very large” above £20m — divided by associated companies, so a company in a group of four is caught at £375,000. A year of grace on first breach if profits stay under £10m; none for very large. Large-company instalments fall in months 7, 10, 13 and 16; very large in months 3, 6, 9 and 12 — entirely within the year being taxed. In the transition year the previous lump sum and a new instalment can land weeks apart.

6 · Losses, VAT, employment costs and EMI

Losses are a rate-arbitrage question

Same period, carried back 12 months, or forward indefinitely — and three years for terminal losses. Nothing is automatic: a claim is needed, and carry-back has a two-year deadline. Post-April 2017 losses can go against total profits, subject to a £5m deductions allowance and 50% above it; the return must specify the allowance, or relief is restricted to 50% even where losses are far below the threshold. Pre-2017 losses are same-trade only and used automatically. A loss relieved at 26.5% is worth more than the same loss relieved at 19%.

VAT registration is a rolling test

£90,000 of taxable turnover on a backward-looking rolling 12 months, checked at each month end, plus a forward test for the next 30 days alone. Deregistration at £88,000. The rolling test catches businesses out because it resets monthly, not annually — notify within 30 days of the month end, registration bites from the first day of the second month after. Early voluntary registration can pay where customers are VAT-registered and there is input VAT to recover on planned capital spend.

Employment costs after April 2025

Employer NIC at 15% and a secondary threshold of £5,000 made every employee and every pay rise dearer. Against that, the Employment Allowance doubled to £10,500 and the £100,000 eligibility cap went, so most SMEs can claim it in full — single-director companies still cannot. It is not automatic and must be claimed through payroll each year; worth confirming rather than assuming. At 15%, pension salary sacrifice saves £150 per £1,000 sacrificed in employer NIC alone.

EMI was widened from April 2026

Employee limit up from 250 to 500 FTE; gross assets from £30m to £120m; company-wide unexercised options from £3m to £6m; maximum exercise period from 10 years to 15, applying to existing options too — though documentation usually needs amending to use it. The individual limit is unchanged at £250,000. No tax on grant, none normally on exercise at market value, and BADR at 18% on sale after two years from grant without needing a 5% holding. Companies previously outside on size grounds should reconsider.

7 · Exit and succession

Business Asset Disposal Relief

Three changes in two years

  • 10% to 5 April 2025, 14% to 5 April 2026, 18% after — on a £1m lifetime limit. Investors’ Relief is aligned to the same schedule.
  • On a £1m qualifying gain the tax cost is now £80,000 higher than before the October 2024 Budget, purely from rate changes.
  • Pre-6 April 2026 contracts can keep the earlier rate, but only as an “excluded contract” — not created for timing advantage, wholly commercial where parties are connected, with a claim to HMRC. Signing before the deadline does not by itself guarantee it.
  • Two-year qualifying period, in every case. Share reorganisations, a new holding company, share class changes or a new shareholder can all restart clocks.

Business Property Relief — £2.5m, not £1m

Much commentary still quotes the old figure

  • From 6 April 2026, 100% BPR and APR is capped at a combined £2.5m per individual. Above that, 50% relief only — an effective 20% inheritance tax rate on the excess.
  • The path to the number. Announced at £1m in Autumn Budget 2024, raised to £2.5m on 23 December 2025, law on Royal Assent to the Finance Act 2026. HMRC’s estimate of affected estates fell from c.2,000 to c.1,100.
  • Transferable between spouses, and refreshing every seven years for individuals and ten for trusts — so £5m for a couple, more with nil-rate bands on top.
  • Anti-forestalling. Lifetime gifts made on or after 30 October 2024 come into the new regime if the donor dies on or after 6 April 2026 within seven years. Settle into more than one trust from that date and a single allowance is shared, not multiplied.
  • Wholly or mainly trading, and two years of ownership. Surplus cash, let property and investment portfolios inside the trading company risk diluting or failing that test.

10 Why culture and experience eats process for breakfast Age, experience and the cultures that turn difference into performance. Process is necessary and insufficient — not optional.

Briefing note · September 2026

It is an odd thing for a finance director to argue that process is not the differentiator. Process is most of what finance people are hired to build: controls, reporting cycles, forecasting discipline, approval limits, month-end. Get those wrong and nothing else you do will save the business.

But process is the floor, not the ceiling. It is also, in almost every case, copyable. A competitor can buy the same ERP, engage the same advisers, adopt the same framework and hire people out of the same firms. Within a year or two they can have a control environment indistinguishable from yours.

What they cannot copy is how people in your business behave when nobody is checking. Whether a twenty-four-year-old will challenge a fifty-eight-year-old in a meeting, and whether the fifty-eight-year-old will change their mind. Whether bad news travels upward quickly or slowly. Whether the best idea wins or the most senior one does.

The argument in short

Process is what stops a business failing. Culture is what makes it win. Confusing the two, in either direction, is expensive. And the honest version of the claim: mixing ages does not improve performance by itself. It improves performance only where the culture converts the difference into something useful. That is precisely the point.

Gallup Q12 meta-analysis: 183,806 business units, 53 industries, 90 countries. Turnover is 21% lower, or 51% lower in low-turnover organisations, and wellbeing 70% higher.

The long-run study, and its caveats

Kotter and Heskett examined more than 200 companies across 22 industries over eleven years to 1990. Firms whose cultures emphasised all key constituencies — customers, shareholders and employees — and encouraged leadership at every level outperformed the rest by a wide margin: revenue growth averaging 682% against 166%, share price growth 901% against 74%, net income growth 756% against 1%. Two caveats rarely travel with those figures. The data ends in 1990, so it describes a different economy. And a study of this design cannot fully separate cause from effect — successful firms can afford better cultures, as well as the reverse. Directionally powerful rather than conclusive.

Where culture actually comes from

Culture is not created by a values statement or an away-day. It is created, or destroyed, by what individual managers do on ordinary Tuesdays. For context on the size of the prize: Gallup puts global engagement at around 20–23%, with the cost of disengagement estimated at $8.8–10 trillion in lost productivity — roughly 9% of global GDP. Most organisations are competing against a very low bar.

2 · The founder-age myth

The dominant story about high-growth business is a young one: the university dropout, the garage, the twenty-three-year-old chief executive. It is a story that shapes who gets funded, who gets promoted and who quietly concludes they are too old to start something. It is also, on the evidence, wrong.

Azoulay, Jones, Kim & Miranda, ‘Age and High-Growth Entrepreneurship’, American Economic Review: Insights — US Census Bureau and IRS records for 2.7 million people who founded businesses between 2007 and 2014 and went on to employ at least one person. By sector the mean was 43.2 in high technology, 42 among venture-backed firms and 45 among patent-holding firms.

The authors estimate a 50-year-old founder is roughly 1.8 times as likely as a 30-year-old to create a top 0.1% growth firm — approximately twice as likely. They found no evidence that founders in their twenties are especially likely to succeed. The mechanism they identify is not age itself but accumulated, specific knowledge: prior experience in the same industry predicts substantially higher rates of success. What looks like an advantage of age is really an advantage of having seen the problem before.

The garage prodigy is not imaginary. But once you look past a small gallery of famous technology companies to millions of real founders, exceptional growth correlates with middle age, not youth.

None of which makes youth a liability. The pattern in the strongest young-founder businesses is consistent: the founder brought something the experienced operator could not, and then deliberately went and found the experience they lacked.

Gymshark

Francis started Gymshark in 2012 at nineteen, screen-printing and sewing garments in a garage in the West Midlands while studying at Aston and delivering pizzas to fund it. The instinct that built the brand — giving free product to fitness YouTubers at an expo, building a community rather than buying advertising — is not an instinct a fifty-five-year-old retail executive would have had.

The more instructive part came next. With revenue around £4.5m and the business straining under its own growth, he brought in Paul Richardson — born 1959, a serial entrepreneur who had built and sold a waste business and helped rescue AllSaints — as executive chairman, and Steve Hewitt as CEO. He stepped down at twenty-three, following honest 360-degree feedback from his own leadership team.

Within roughly three years sales passed £100m and pre-tax profits reached £18m. In 2020 General Atlantic took a 21% stake above £1bn — only the second UK business to reach unicorn status with no prior external funding. Revenue reached £646m in 2025.

Why it matters

No process compelled him to step down. It was a cultural act — a founder concluding, in public and at some cost to his ego, that the business mattered more than his title.

Revolut

Storonsky, born 1984, founded Revolut in 2015 after trading derivatives at Lehman Brothers and Credit Suisse. In January 2020 the company appointed its first chairman: Martin Gilbert, born 1955, then sixty-four — an MA in accountancy, chartered accountant with Deloitte, co-founder of Aberdeen Asset Management in 1983 and its leader for thirty-four years to the Standard Life merger. He had chaired the PRA's Practitioner Panel: precisely the regulatory terrain a fintech seeking a banking licence was about to cross.

Why it matters

A twenty-nine-year age gap where the older party's value was not general wisdom but a precise understanding of regulators, capital and governance that no amount of energy substitutes for.

Alphabet and Stripe

Page and Brin were in their mid-twenties in 1998 and brought in Eric Schmidt as chief executive in 2001, in an arrangement widely described at the time as adult supervision. The finance version came later: Ruth Porat joined as CFO in May 2015 after twenty-seven years at Morgan Stanley, where she had been CFO through the post-crisis restructuring and had advised the US Treasury on Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and AIG. She was credited with imposing discipline on spending across Alphabet's speculative Other Bets.

Stripe followed the same route. The Collisons founded it in 2010 at twenty-one and nineteen; in 2020 they recruited Dhivya Suryadevara as CFO from General Motors — a company founded in 1908, with $137bn of revenue and 164,000 employees against Stripe's 2,800 at the time. John Collison's public rationale was that she had run an industry-leading giant while still being energised by products that did not yet exist.

Why it matters

In every case the young founder retained what they were genuinely better at, and bought in what they were not.

4 · The uncomfortable finding: mixing ages is not enough

Here the commentary and the evidence part company, and it is worth being straight about it, because the gap is the whole argument. Age diversity, on its own, does not reliably improve performance.

What the research actually found

Three independent bodies of work

  • 74 studies reviewed against performance quality, financial performance, innovation, effectiveness, satisfaction and turnover. Age diversity was reliably related to only one: turnover. The authors present this explicitly as counter-evidence to the claims usually made.
  • 745 natural teams, 8,848 employees, six years, across car production, administration and financial services, with a representative survey of 2,000 workers: mixed-age teams show significant advantages and significant disadvantages.
  • A large employer-employee panel study reached the sharpest version: age diversity lifts productivity if and only if the work is creative rather than routine. In routine-task businesses it may actively hinder it.

The conditions under which it pays

Every one cultural, not procedural

  • High task complexity — difficult and novel enough for different perspectives to add value
  • Low salience of age — differences present, but not the first thing people notice about each other
  • High appreciation of age diversity — positively valuing what different generations bring, rather than tolerating it
  • A positive team climate
  • Low age discrimination
  • Age-differentiated leadership — managers who adapt how they lead to who they are leading

The whole point

You can appoint an experienced non-executive tomorrow morning. That is governance. Whether anybody listens to them, and whether they listen back, is culture — and it does not appear in the board minutes.

Difference is raw material. Culture is the machinery that converts it into performance, and without the machinery the raw material sits there, or actively causes friction — which is exactly what the turnover finding suggests is happening in a lot of organisations.

5 · What this means for an owner-managed business

The businesses above are not SMEs. But the mechanism scales down more cleanly than most management research does, because in a smaller business the founder's behaviour is the culture — there are not enough layers for it to be anything else.

Make values behavioural, not abstract

Integrity, excellence and teamwork are not values; they are nouns no one would argue against. A value only does work when it is specific enough to rule something out. “We tell customers about problems before they find them” is a value, because it forbids something otherwise tempting. The test: name the last time it cost the business money to honour it. If no one can, it is decoration.

Accept that the manager is the culture

If roughly 70% of the variance in engagement traces to the manager, culture work that stops at the leadership team is missing most of the effect. In a business of forty people, four or five individuals genuinely determine whether the culture is real. Promoting the best technician into management without preparing them is the most common way owner-managed businesses accidentally dismantle their own culture.

Buy experience in the shape you need

The underlying need — someone who has seen this problem before — does not always require a full-time executive hire. For most SMEs the practical options are a non-executive director, a chair, or fractional senior finance support. What matters is not the contract; it is whether the person has genuinely done the specific thing the business is about to attempt, and whether the culture will let them say something unwelcome.

Design the work so difference pays

Age diversity pays in complex, creative work and can hinder routine work. Putting a twenty-five-year-old and a fifty-five-year-old on the same repetitive process may achieve nothing. Putting them together on a pricing question, a new market or a product decision is where the evidence says the value sits.

Make the exchange run both ways

The founder-and-veteran pattern is usually described as the young learning from the old. In the successful examples it runs both ways: Francis retained control of brand and product, where his instincts were better than his chairman's, while ceding operations. Reverse mentoring formalises the same idea. The cultural signal — that expertise is not a function of age — is worth more than the content transferred.

Measure it, or it is a slogan

Engagement scores, voluntary turnover by tenure and age band, absence rates, internal promotion rates, exit interview themes and the ratio of internal referrals to external hires are all available, cheap to track and leading indicators of financial performance. A finance function that reports on none of them is measuring only the lagging half of the business.

6 · Where this argument gets misused

Culture as an excuse for absent control

A business with strong values and weak controls is not a well-run business; it is a well-liked one waiting for an incident. Culture does not reconcile a bank account, detect a fraud, or produce a covenant certificate. The argument here is that process is necessary and insufficient — not that it is optional. A founder who resists financial discipline on the grounds that it would compromise the culture has usually misunderstood both.

The gap between stated and lived values

Several high-profile founder-led businesses have had their published culture publicly contradicted by former employees, and the reputational damage has been severe precisely because the claims were loud. A business that says little about its culture and behaves well is in a far stronger position than one that does the reverse. Publishing values raises the standard you will be held to; it does not meet it.

There is also an honest limit to the evidence itself. Culture research struggles with reverse causality throughout — profitable businesses can afford to treat people well. The most defensible reading is that culture and performance reinforce each other, and that the direction of travel is easier to change early than late.

The practical checklist

  • Write down your values as behaviours that forbid something, then identify the last occasion each one cost you money.
  • Map the age and experience profile of your leadership team and your board. Note the gaps rather than congratulating yourself on the range.
  • Identify which of your problems are genuinely complex and novel, and staff those deliberately with mixed experience levels.
  • Assess whether your managers — the four or five people who actually set the tone — have been prepared for the role or simply promoted into it.
  • Decide what experience the business is missing, and buy it in the appropriate shape: non-executive, chair or fractional. Do not default to a full-time hire.
  • Establish whether the culture allows an experienced outsider to say something unwelcome, before you appoint one.
  • Put four or five culture metrics into the monthly board pack alongside the financials, and track them long enough to see trends.
  • Ask the youngest people in the business what the leadership team is wrong about, and create a route for the answer to travel.

11 Unfair dismissal from 1 January 2027 The qualifying period falls to six months and the cap on compensation is abolished. A bounded liability with a known ceiling becomes an open-ended one — at the point when the queue to find out has never been longer.

Technical briefing · September 2026

On 1 January 2027 the qualifying period for ordinary unfair dismissal falls from two years to six months, and the cap on the compensatory award is abolished altogether. Both changes come from the Employment Rights Act 2025, which received Royal Assent on 18 December 2025 and is being commenced in stages across 2026 and 2027.

Most commentary treats this as a human resources problem. It is also a financial one. A contested dismissal ceases to be a bounded liability with a known ceiling and becomes an open-ended contingent liability that, given current tribunal waiting times, may remain unresolved for two to four years. That is a provisioning question and a due diligence question as much as an employment question.

The change in one line

It is not that dismissal becomes harder. It is that the consequence of getting it wrong stops having a ceiling, at exactly the point when the queue to find out has never been longer.

The changes apply in England, Scotland and Wales. Unfair dismissal is devolved in Northern Ireland, where a separate package of reform is planned.

Section 25 reduces the qualifying period to six months and also removes the power to vary that period by secondary legislation — any future change requires primary legislation, which gives employers more planning certainty than the position that has applied since the period moved to two years in April 2012. Schedule 3 makes the corresponding reduction for written reasons and removes the qualifying period entirely for spent-conviction dismissals. Section 124 of the Employment Rights Act 1996, which contains the cap, is repealed in full. The government has confirmed it does not intend to consult further, and the dates should be treated as firm.

2 · Commencement mechanics — who is actually caught

01

There is no phase-in

From 1 January 2027, any employee who already has six months’ service is protected immediately. No transitional ramp, no grandfathering of existing staff. In practical terms, anyone engaged from 1 July 2026 onwards who is still employed on 1 January 2027 will have the necessary service. Everyone hired before that date is covered from day one of the new regime.

02

Statutory notice counts as service, whether or not you give it

The effective date of termination is extended by the statutory minimum notice period, regardless of whether that notice was given or worked. Between one month and two years’ service, the statutory minimum is one week.

So an employee dismissed in late December 2026 without working notice may have their termination date pushed into January 2027, and qualify under the six-month rule rather than the two-year rule. Broadly, around twenty-five weeks of actual service will be enough.

Act on this

Any dismissal planned for December 2026 needs this checked before notice is given.

03

The two changes commence differently

They are usually described as one package, but they do not bite on identical populations. The uncapped regime applies only where the actual date of termination falls on or after 1 January 2027. An employee cannot rely on an extended effective date to reach it, even though an extended date can carry them into the six-month qualifying period.

A December 2026 dismissal could therefore give rise to a claim that would not otherwise have existed — but that claim would still be subject to the cap. The distinction is narrow and worth confirming with an adviser in any specific case.

3 · What does not change

  • Day-one protection against discrimination is unaffected, as is day-one protection against automatically unfair dismissal — whistleblowing, trade union activity, health and safety, asserting a statutory right, and the rest.
  • The basis of compensation is unchanged. Awards remain based on actual and projected loss, evidenced by the claimant, subject to what the tribunal considers just and equitable. Removing the cap does not create a new head of loss; it removes a ceiling.
  • Notice periods are unchanged, both statutory and contractual.
  • Non-renewal of a fixed-term contract on expiry remains a dismissal. Where the employee has six months’ service, non-renewal will need to be a fair dismissal — materially affecting businesses running seasonal or project-based arrangements.
  • Protection still applies only to employees, not to workers or the genuinely self-employed. Wider employment status reform is under discussion but nothing is legislated.

4 · How this affects an employer’s current position

Probation periods no longer function as a buffer

A live change, not a theoretical one

A six-month probation period — the most common length in UK practice — will now expire at almost exactly the moment protection begins. Allowing for the statutory notice extension, protection may in fact arrive slightly before probation ends. A probation framework designed on the old assumptions is now actively working against the employer.

Legal commentary has converged on three to four months, with a clearly drafted and sparingly used right to extend by around a month, to force the assessment decision to a point where there is still room to act. Around 40% of businesses operate a discretionary probationary period, and the substantial majority are six months or shorter.

Performance management has to become earlier and evidenced

Not a new standard — a much larger group

A concern identified at month four or five could previously be handled with some informality. Now, delay risks the employee acquiring protection before any decision is reached. The requirement is documented objectives from the start, recorded review conversations at defined points, and a decision taken deliberately rather than by drift.

This is the same fair-process standard that has always applied at two years’ service. Businesses that already manage performance properly face little additional exposure; those that have relied on the qualifying period as a substitute for management face a great deal.

senior managers, long notice periods, bonus or share-based earnings, long periods out of the market

Removing the cap matters far less than the headlines suggest for typical cases, and far more for a specific group. It becomes the operative constraint precisely where a finance director should be looking: the senior population where a single claim can become material.

A secondary effect is on settlement dynamics. Where a cap exists, both sides negotiate against a known worst case. Removing it widens the range of credible outcomes and, at the margin, increases the price of settling — particularly for higher earners.

This is a balance sheet question, not only an HR one

Employment litigation that used to be a note in due diligence becomes a number.

Given tribunal waiting times, a contested dismissal of a senior employee now represents an uncapped contingent liability that may sit unresolved across two or three reporting periods — with consequences for provisioning, audit discussion, disclosure, and warranty and disclosure schedules on any transaction.

5 · Why this is good for employees

6.3 million people

22% of everyone employed aged 16 and over have been with their current employer for between six months and two years. Under current law that group has only very limited protection: they can be dismissed without a fair reason and without a fair process, with no recourse unless the dismissal is discriminatory or automatically unfair. From January 2027 they gain the right to a fair reason and a fair process, the right to written reasons, and access to a tribunal if neither is provided. The government’s framing is the reduction of one-sided flexibility, and on that measure the change does what it claims.

A less obvious second effect

The government’s stated rationale for removing the cap is that it created an incentive for claimants to construct more complex cases pleading both unfair dismissal and discrimination, because discrimination awards are uncapped. Removing the cap on ordinary unfair dismissal reduces that incentive, which should mean simpler claims and faster determinations. There is also a point about security rather than litigation: the great majority of employees never bring a claim, and the value of the protection lies in the behaviour it induces — better process, earlier feedback, clearer reasons.

6 · Is this good or bad for employers?

Both, and the balance depends almost entirely on how well the business is already run.

The case that it is manageable, or even positive

  • The final position is considerably better than the original proposal. Day-one unfair dismissal rights were in the Bill as published in October 2024; defeats in the House of Lords produced the six-month compromise. Six months preserves a genuine assessment window.
  • Nothing here is difficult for an employer that already documents objectives, holds recorded reviews and follows the Acas Code. The discipline required ought to exist regardless.
  • The six-month figure is comparatively stable, since the power to change it by secondary legislation has gone.
  • Better early-stage performance management tends to improve retention and reduce the cost of mis-hires — a real if unquantified offset.
  • Fewer bolted-on discrimination claims, if the government is right about the incentive the cap created. Those are the most expensive kind to defend.

The case that it is a material burden

  • Senior dismissal exposure is genuinely uncapped for the first time, and the removal of the cap was neither consulted on nor expected.
  • Compliance cost falls disproportionately on smaller businesses. The government’s own impact assessment acknowledged proportionately higher costs for small and micro businesses, given the fixed nature of administrative burdens.
  • It arrives alongside a very large volume of other change — more than thirty separate measures across 2026 and 2027. The aggregate burden is greater than any single item suggests.
  • It lands on a tribunal system already failing to keep pace, so the cost of defending even a claim you win rises with the length of the queue.
  • The scale of the cost is contested. The initial impact assessment estimated under £5bn a year to business; a revised assessment following the concessions put it at around £1bn. The TUC argues the central estimate was always about £1bn. The British Chambers of Commerce calls £1bn a substantial underestimate for excluding management time. The Regulatory Policy Committee found eight of the twenty-three individual impact assessments not fit for purpose. A CIPD survey of 2,000 employers found roughly eight in ten expected higher employment costs and about half expected a negative effect on employment in their own organisation.

An honest assessment

The change redistributes risk from employees to employers, and it is legitimate to describe that as a cost. But the specific cost falls hardest on employers who were using the two-year qualifying period as an alternative to managing people, and very lightly on those who were not. For a well-run business with documented processes, the principal new exposure is the uncapped award on senior dismissals — which is a real risk, and a quantifiable one, and therefore a manageable one. The genuine systemic concern is not the legal change. It is that the volume of claims is rising into a tribunal system that cannot currently absorb the claims it already has.

Hearings in some regions, notably London and the south east, are reported as being listed into 2030. Acas early conciliation is under corresponding pressure, receiving more than 135,000 notifications between April 2025 and February 2026. From 1 October 2026 the time limit for bringing most claims doubles from three months to six, lengthening the period during which a dismissal remains a live risk before the employer even knows whether a claim will be brought.

The cost of a contested dismissal is no longer primarily the award. It is legal fees, management time and uncertainty spread across several years, whatever the outcome.

8 · Timeline: what has already changed

Date What changed
18 Dec 2025Employment Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent. The Strikes (Minimum Service Levels) Act 2023 was repealed the same day.
18 Feb 2026First trade union package: 40% support threshold for industrial action in important public services removed; ballot and notice information requirements reverted to the simpler pre-2016 form; notice of industrial action cut from 14 to 10 days; mandate period extended from 6 to 12 months; picket supervision requirements repealed; protection against dismissal for taking industrial action introduced; political fund opt-out and 10-year ballot rules repealed; public sector check-off and facility time obligations repealed.
6 Apr 2026Statutory sick pay reformed — the lower earnings limit and the three waiting days both removed, so SSP is payable from day one, with employees below the LEL entitled to 80% of wages.
6 Apr 2026Day-one right to paternity leave and unpaid parental leave. Bereaved partner’s paternity leave introduced as a day-one right of up to 52 weeks where the mother or primary caregiver dies.
6 Apr 2026Whistleblowing protection extended to cover complaints of sexual harassment.
6 Apr 2026Maximum protective award for failure to consult on collective redundancy doubled from 90 to 180 days’ pay.
6 Apr 2026New duty to keep records of both annual leave entitlement and holiday pay, retained for six years. Failure to comply is an offence punishable by a fine. Introduced without prior notice and widely missed.
6 Apr 2026Gender pay gap and menopause ‘equality action plans’ available on a voluntary basis. Simplified trade union recognition mechanisms took effect.
7 Apr 2026The Fair Work Agency became operational, combining HMRC’s national minimum wage enforcement, the Gangmasters and Labour Abuse Authority and the Employment Agency Standards Inspectorate.
Aug 2026Electronic balloting for industrial action introduced and the 50% ballot turnout threshold repealed. A new Code of Practice on electronic and workplace balloting came into force at the end of August; unions may now use postal, electronic or hybrid voting, and workplace voting where the employer agrees.

11 · Action checklist

  • Review the length and drafting of your probation period. If it is six months, it no longer functions as intended — consider three to four months with a clear, limited right to extend.
  • Identify every employee who will pass six months’ service between now and 1 January 2027, and make sure any decision about their suitability is taken with time to spare.
  • Check any dismissal planned for December 2026 against the statutory notice extension before notice is given.
  • Put documented objectives and recorded review points into the first six months of every role, and train line managers on what a fair process requires.
  • Review fixed-term contract arrangements, particularly seasonal and project-based ones, since non-renewal at expiry is a dismissal.
  • Identify the senior roles where an uncapped award would be material, and consider whether that exposure should be provisioned, insured or reflected in transaction disclosures.
  • Update employee handbooks and policy documents for the tribunal time limit change from three to six months.
  • Confirm you are keeping records of annual leave entitlement as well as holiday pay, retained for six years, as required since 6 April 2026.
  • Map the remaining 2026 and 2027 measures against your own policies and diarise the ones that need action.
  • Take independent legal advice before updating employment policies, and before any dismissal of an employee with more than six months’ service once the new regime is in force.
