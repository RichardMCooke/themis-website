Unfair dismissal from 1 January 2027

The qualifying period falls to six months and the cap on compensation is abolished. A bounded liability with a known ceiling becomes an open-ended one — at the point when the queue to find out has never been longer.

Briefing

Technical briefing · September 2026

On 1 January 2027 the qualifying period for ordinary unfair dismissal falls from two years to six months, and the cap on the compensatory award is abolished altogether. Both changes come from the Employment Rights Act 2025, which received Royal Assent on 18 December 2025 and is being commenced in stages across 2026 and 2027.

Most commentary treats this as a human resources problem. It is also a financial one. A contested dismissal ceases to be a bounded liability with a known ceiling and becomes an open-ended contingent liability that, given current tribunal waiting times, may remain unresolved for two to four years. That is a provisioning question and a due diligence question as much as an employment question.

The change in one line

It is not that dismissal becomes harder. It is that the consequence of getting it wrong stops having a ceiling, at exactly the point when the queue to find out has never been longer.

The changes apply in England, Scotland and Wales. Unfair dismissal is devolved in Northern Ireland, where a separate package of reform is planned.

Section 25 reduces the qualifying period to six months and also removes the power to vary that period by secondary legislation — any future change requires primary legislation, which gives employers more planning certainty than the position that has applied since the period moved to two years in April 2012. Schedule 3 makes the corresponding reduction for written reasons and removes the qualifying period entirely for spent-conviction dismissals. Section 124 of the Employment Rights Act 1996, which contains the cap, is repealed in full. The government has confirmed it does not intend to consult further, and the dates should be treated as firm.

2 · Commencement mechanics — who is actually caught

01

There is no phase-in

From 1 January 2027, any employee who already has six months’ service is protected immediately. No transitional ramp, no grandfathering of existing staff. In practical terms, anyone engaged from 1 July 2026 onwards who is still employed on 1 January 2027 will have the necessary service. Everyone hired before that date is covered from day one of the new regime.

02

Statutory notice counts as service, whether or not you give it

The effective date of termination is extended by the statutory minimum notice period, regardless of whether that notice was given or worked. Between one month and two years’ service, the statutory minimum is one week.

So an employee dismissed in late December 2026 without working notice may have their termination date pushed into January 2027, and qualify under the six-month rule rather than the two-year rule. Broadly, around twenty-five weeks of actual service will be enough.

Act on this

Any dismissal planned for December 2026 needs this checked before notice is given.

03

The two changes commence differently

They are usually described as one package, but they do not bite on identical populations. The uncapped regime applies only where the actual date of termination falls on or after 1 January 2027. An employee cannot rely on an extended effective date to reach it, even though an extended date can carry them into the six-month qualifying period.

A December 2026 dismissal could therefore give rise to a claim that would not otherwise have existed — but that claim would still be subject to the cap. The distinction is narrow and worth confirming with an adviser in any specific case.

3 · What does not change

  • Day-one protection against discrimination is unaffected, as is day-one protection against automatically unfair dismissal — whistleblowing, trade union activity, health and safety, asserting a statutory right, and the rest.
  • The basis of compensation is unchanged. Awards remain based on actual and projected loss, evidenced by the claimant, subject to what the tribunal considers just and equitable. Removing the cap does not create a new head of loss; it removes a ceiling.
  • Notice periods are unchanged, both statutory and contractual.
  • Non-renewal of a fixed-term contract on expiry remains a dismissal. Where the employee has six months’ service, non-renewal will need to be a fair dismissal — materially affecting businesses running seasonal or project-based arrangements.
  • Protection still applies only to employees, not to workers or the genuinely self-employed. Wider employment status reform is under discussion but nothing is legislated.

4 · How this affects an employer’s current position

Probation periods no longer function as a buffer

A live change, not a theoretical one

A six-month probation period — the most common length in UK practice — will now expire at almost exactly the moment protection begins. Allowing for the statutory notice extension, protection may in fact arrive slightly before probation ends. A probation framework designed on the old assumptions is now actively working against the employer.

Legal commentary has converged on three to four months, with a clearly drafted and sparingly used right to extend by around a month, to force the assessment decision to a point where there is still room to act. Around 40% of businesses operate a discretionary probationary period, and the substantial majority are six months or shorter.

Performance management has to become earlier and evidenced

Not a new standard — a much larger group

A concern identified at month four or five could previously be handled with some informality. Now, delay risks the employee acquiring protection before any decision is reached. The requirement is documented objectives from the start, recorded review conversations at defined points, and a decision taken deliberately rather than by drift.

This is the same fair-process standard that has always applied at two years’ service. Businesses that already manage performance properly face little additional exposure; those that have relied on the qualifying period as a substitute for management face a great deal.

Removing the cap matters far less than the headlines suggest for typical cases, and far more for a specific group. It becomes the operative constraint precisely where a finance director should be looking: the senior population where a single claim can become material.

A secondary effect is on settlement dynamics. Where a cap exists, both sides negotiate against a known worst case. Removing it widens the range of credible outcomes and, at the margin, increases the price of settling — particularly for higher earners.

This is a balance sheet question, not only an HR one

Employment litigation that used to be a note in due diligence becomes a number.

Given tribunal waiting times, a contested dismissal of a senior employee now represents an uncapped contingent liability that may sit unresolved across two or three reporting periods — with consequences for provisioning, audit discussion, disclosure, and warranty and disclosure schedules on any transaction.

5 · Why this is good for employees

6.3 million people

22% of everyone employed aged 16 and over have been with their current employer for between six months and two years. Under current law that group has only very limited protection: they can be dismissed without a fair reason and without a fair process, with no recourse unless the dismissal is discriminatory or automatically unfair. From January 2027 they gain the right to a fair reason and a fair process, the right to written reasons, and access to a tribunal if neither is provided. The government’s framing is the reduction of one-sided flexibility, and on that measure the change does what it claims.

A less obvious second effect

The government’s stated rationale for removing the cap is that it created an incentive for claimants to construct more complex cases pleading both unfair dismissal and discrimination, because discrimination awards are uncapped. Removing the cap on ordinary unfair dismissal reduces that incentive, which should mean simpler claims and faster determinations. There is also a point about security rather than litigation: the great majority of employees never bring a claim, and the value of the protection lies in the behaviour it induces — better process, earlier feedback, clearer reasons.

6 · Is this good or bad for employers?

Both, and the balance depends almost entirely on how well the business is already run.

The case that it is manageable, or even positive

  • The final position is considerably better than the original proposal. Day-one unfair dismissal rights were in the Bill as published in October 2024; defeats in the House of Lords produced the six-month compromise. Six months preserves a genuine assessment window.
  • Nothing here is difficult for an employer that already documents objectives, holds recorded reviews and follows the Acas Code. The discipline required ought to exist regardless.
  • The six-month figure is comparatively stable, since the power to change it by secondary legislation has gone.
  • Better early-stage performance management tends to improve retention and reduce the cost of mis-hires — a real if unquantified offset.
  • Fewer bolted-on discrimination claims, if the government is right about the incentive the cap created. Those are the most expensive kind to defend.

The case that it is a material burden

  • Senior dismissal exposure is genuinely uncapped for the first time, and the removal of the cap was neither consulted on nor expected.
  • Compliance cost falls disproportionately on smaller businesses. The government’s own impact assessment acknowledged proportionately higher costs for small and micro businesses, given the fixed nature of administrative burdens.
  • It arrives alongside a very large volume of other change — more than thirty separate measures across 2026 and 2027. The aggregate burden is greater than any single item suggests.
  • It lands on a tribunal system already failing to keep pace, so the cost of defending even a claim you win rises with the length of the queue.
  • The scale of the cost is contested. The initial impact assessment estimated under £5bn a year to business; a revised assessment following the concessions put it at around £1bn. The TUC argues the central estimate was always about £1bn. The British Chambers of Commerce calls £1bn a substantial underestimate for excluding management time. The Regulatory Policy Committee found eight of the twenty-three individual impact assessments not fit for purpose. A CIPD survey of 2,000 employers found roughly eight in ten expected higher employment costs and about half expected a negative effect on employment in their own organisation.

An honest assessment

The change redistributes risk from employees to employers, and it is legitimate to describe that as a cost. But the specific cost falls hardest on employers who were using the two-year qualifying period as an alternative to managing people, and very lightly on those who were not. For a well-run business with documented processes, the principal new exposure is the uncapped award on senior dismissals — which is a real risk, and a quantifiable one, and therefore a manageable one. The genuine systemic concern is not the legal change. It is that the volume of claims is rising into a tribunal system that cannot currently absorb the claims it already has.

Hearings in some regions, notably London and the south east, are reported as being listed into 2030. Complex ‘open track’ claims — typically including discrimination or whistleblowing allegations — now account for around 61% of single claims nationally. Acas early conciliation is under corresponding pressure, receiving more than 135,000 notifications between April 2025 and February 2026. From 1 October 2026 the time limit for bringing most claims doubles from three months to six, lengthening the period during which a dismissal remains a live risk before the employer even knows whether a claim will be brought.

The cost of a contested dismissal is no longer primarily the award. It is legal fees, management time and uncertainty spread across several years, whatever the outcome.

8 · Timeline: what has already changed

Date What changed
18 Dec 2025Employment Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent. The Strikes (Minimum Service Levels) Act 2023 was repealed the same day.
18 Feb 2026First trade union package: 40% support threshold for industrial action in important public services removed; ballot and notice information requirements reverted to the simpler pre-2016 form; notice of industrial action cut from 14 to 10 days; mandate period extended from 6 to 12 months; picket supervision requirements repealed; protection against dismissal for taking industrial action introduced; political fund opt-out and 10-year ballot rules repealed; public sector check-off and facility time obligations repealed.
6 Apr 2026Statutory sick pay reformed — the lower earnings limit and the three waiting days both removed, so SSP is payable from day one, with employees below the LEL entitled to 80% of wages.
6 Apr 2026Day-one right to paternity leave and unpaid parental leave. Bereaved partner’s paternity leave introduced as a day-one right of up to 52 weeks where the mother or primary caregiver dies.
6 Apr 2026Whistleblowing protection extended to cover complaints of sexual harassment.
6 Apr 2026Maximum protective award for failure to consult on collective redundancy doubled from 90 to 180 days’ pay.
6 Apr 2026New duty to keep records of both annual leave entitlement and holiday pay, retained for six years. Failure to comply is an offence punishable by a fine. Introduced without prior notice and widely missed.
6 Apr 2026Gender pay gap and menopause ‘equality action plans’ available on a voluntary basis. Simplified trade union recognition mechanisms took effect.
7 Apr 2026The Fair Work Agency became operational, combining HMRC’s national minimum wage enforcement, the Gangmasters and Labour Abuse Authority and the Employment Agency Standards Inspectorate.
Aug 2026Electronic balloting for industrial action introduced and the 50% ballot turnout threshold repealed. A new Code of Practice on electronic and workplace balloting came into force at the end of August; unions may now use postal, electronic or hybrid voting, and workplace voting where the employer agrees.

10 · Timeline: 2027

Date What changes
1 Jan 2027Unfair dismissal: qualifying period cut from 2 years to 6 months; written reasons for dismissal at 6 months; qualifying period for spent-conviction dismissals removed; the cap on the compensatory award abolished.
1 Jan 2027Fire and rehire: defined categories of ‘restricted variations’ — including detrimental changes to pay, pensions, hours, shifts and holidays — where dismissal and re-engagement on new terms is automatically unfair unless a financial necessity test is met. Pushed back from October 2026.

During 2027Zero-hours and low-hours reforms: right to a contract reflecting hours regularly worked, reasonable notice of shifts, and compensation for shifts cancelled or curtailed at short notice, extending to agency workers. Consultation closed 25 August 2026.
During 2027Flexible working: employers will only be able to refuse a request where a statutory ground applies and the refusal is reasonable.
During 2027Collective redundancy: new employer-wide ‘establishment’ test, removing the separate-site analysis.

11 · Action checklist

  • Review the length and drafting of your probation period. If it is six months, it no longer functions as intended — consider three to four months with a clear, limited right to extend.
  • Identify every employee who will pass six months’ service between now and 1 January 2027, and make sure any decision about their suitability is taken with time to spare.
  • Check any dismissal planned for December 2026 against the statutory notice extension before notice is given.
  • Put documented objectives and recorded review points into the first six months of every role, and train line managers on what a fair process requires.
  • Review fixed-term contract arrangements, particularly seasonal and project-based ones, since non-renewal at expiry is a dismissal.
  • Identify the senior roles where an uncapped award would be material, and consider whether that exposure should be provisioned, insured or reflected in transaction disclosures.
  • Update employee handbooks and policy documents for the tribunal time limit change from three to six months.
  • Confirm you are keeping records of annual leave entitlement as well as holiday pay, retained for six years, as required since 6 April 2026.
  • Map the remaining 2026 and 2027 measures against your own policies and diarise the ones that need action.
  • Take independent legal advice before updating employment policies, and before any dismissal of an employee with more than six months’ service once the new regime is in force.
