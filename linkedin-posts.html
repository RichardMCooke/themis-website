01 IFRS 18 is coming: what growing businesses need to know (and do now) The biggest change to how companies present their performance in over 20 years. Profit doesn't change — how you show it, explain it and back it up does.

Published 1 November 2026 · Article

If your business reports under IFRS, your income statement is about to look different. IFRS 18 Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements replaces IAS 1 for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, and is widely described as the biggest change to how companies present their performance in more than 20 years.

The reassuring part first: IFRS 18 does not change how much profit you make. Recognition and measurement stay the same, and the balance sheet barely moves. What changes is how your results are structured, labelled and explained, and the data your finance team needs to produce them.

The less reassuring part: the standard applies retrospectively, so a December year-end business will need to restate its 2026 comparatives. In other words, the work starts a full year before the first IFRS 18 year end, not at it.

First things first: does IFRS 18 apply to you?

If your company prepares its accounts under FRS 102, IFRS 18 does not apply to your statutory accounts. That covers most UK SMEs, so it is worth saying plainly before anything else.

But plenty of growing businesses are pulled into the IFRS world in other ways. IFRS 18 is likely to matter to you if:

  • You report under UK-adopted IFRS. That includes AIM-quoted groups and private companies that have chosen IFRS. The UK Endorsement Board endorsed IFRS 18 for UK use on 10 December 2025.
  • You are a subsidiary of a group that reports under IFRS. Even if your own accounts are FRS 102, your parent will need your group reporting pack in the new IFRS 18 shape.
  • You use FRS 101, the reduced disclosure framework. FRS 101 is built on IFRS, so the new income statement structure reaches you. The FRC has granted some relief: FRS 101 reporters are exempt from the new performance-measure disclosures and from part of the new expense-by-nature analysis.
  • You are heading for a sale, new investor or refinancing. An IFRS-reporting acquirer, private equity house or lender will look at your numbers through the IFRS 18 lens, whatever framework you use today.

Not sure which box you sit in? That is a ten-minute conversation, and a good place to start.

The six changes that matter most

IFRS 18 runs to well over a hundred paragraphs, but for an owner-managed or mid-sized business these six do the heavy lifting.

01

A new shape for the income statement

Every line of income and expense must sit in one of five categories: operating, investing, financing, income taxes or discontinued operations. Operating is the default home for anything that does not belong elsewhere.

02

Two new required subtotals

You must show operating profit and profit before financing and income taxes. For the first time, “operating profit” has a fixed definition, so your new figure may not match the one you report today.

03

Your adjusted profit figures come under the spotlight

If you quote measures such as adjusted EBITDA or underlying operating profit in public communications, IFRS 18 calls these management-defined performance measures. They must be explained in a single note in the audited accounts: why you use them, how you calculate them, and a reconciliation to the nearest IFRS subtotal, showing the tax and minority-interest effect of each adjustment.

04

No more hiding in “other”

New grouping principles mean material items must be broken out, and “other” may only be used when no more informative label exists. Large sundry lines will be challenged.

05

More detail behind your cost lines

If you present expenses by function (cost of sales, distribution, admin), you must disclose how much depreciation, amortisation, employee benefits, impairment losses and inventory write-downs sit within each line. Many ledgers do not capture that today.

06

A tidier, but different, cash flow statement

Under the indirect method, the starting point becomes operating profit. For most trading businesses, interest paid must now be shown in financing and interest and dividends received in investing, with the old policy choices removed. Your reported operating cash flow may change even though your bank balance does not.

The balance sheet sees only minor changes.

Why the work starts early

IFRS 18 applies retrospectively. A business with a 31 December year end will publish its first IFRS 18 accounts for 2027, but the 2026 comparatives must be restated in the new format too.

The standard also asks for something extra in that first year: a reconciliation, line by line, between the 2026 income statement as originally presented under IAS 1 and the restated IFRS 18 version. Groups that publish half-year reports under IAS 34 face this sooner, in their first interim report of 2027.

IFRS 18: the key dates

For a business with a 31 December year end

IFRS 18 applies retrospectively: 2026 figures must be restated, with a line-by-line reconciliation to the old presentation.

In practice, that means the data you capture during 2026 needs to support the new categories and disclosures. Mapping it as you go is far easier than reconstructing it from an old trial balance in 2028.

More than a formatting exercise

It is tempting to treat IFRS 18 as a job for the year-end accounts template. The businesses that handle it well will look further, because the changes ripple into everyday finance:

  • Chart of accounts and ledger coding. Each nominal code needs a home in the new categories, and some may need splitting to capture expenses by nature.
  • Reporting and consolidation tools. Templates, mappings and group reporting packs will need rebuilding, and tested before the first year end.
  • Banking covenants. Check whether your facility definitions refer to reported operating profit or operating cash flow, or are protected by a “frozen GAAP” clause. A presentation change could move a covenant calculation without anything real changing.
  • Bonus schemes, earn-outs and share plans. Targets linked to “operating profit” may need clarifying before the definition shifts underneath them.
  • Board packs and KPIs. Aligning management reporting with the new subtotals avoids running two versions of the truth.
  • Your auditor. Agreeing classification judgements early avoids a difficult conversation at year end.

02 Why culture and experience eats process for breakfast Odd thing for a CFO to say. Process is the floor, not the ceiling — and it's copyable. How your people behave when nobody's checking is not.

Posted 6 September 2026 · Post

Odd thing for a CFO to say. So let me be clear: process matters. Controls, reporting, forecasting discipline — get those wrong and nothing else saves you.

But process is the floor, not the ceiling. It's also copyable. A competitor can buy the same ERP, hire the same advisers, adopt the same framework. Within two years their control environment looks like yours.

What they can't copy is how your people behave when nobody's checking. Whether a 24-year-old will challenge a 58-year-old — and whether the 58-year-old changes their mind.

Start with who actually builds high-growth firms

A US Census study of 2.7 million founders found the fastest-growing 0.1% of new ventures had founders averaging 45 at launch. Not 25. And age rose the further into the top tail you looked.

Mean founder age by growth band

All new firms 41.9
Top 1% growth 43.7
Top 0.1% growth 45.0
Successful exit 46.7

Azoulay, Jones, Kim & Miranda, ‘Age and High-Growth Entrepreneurship’, American Economic Review: Insights. A 50-year-old was roughly twice as likely as a 30-year-old to build a top 0.1% firm.

Yet the best young-founder businesses did something specific

They went and found the experience they lacked.

Business The young founder The experienced hand

Revenue was c.£4.5m; within three years sales passed £100m and pre-tax profit hit £18m. £646m in 2025.

Now the part that gets left out

Mixing ages does nothing on its own. A review of 74 studies found age diversity reliably predicted exactly one outcome: turnover. German research across 745 teams and 8,848 employees found mixed-age teams carry real advantages and real disadvantages — and the advantages only appear under specific conditions. Complex work. Positive team climate. Low age discrimination. Genuine appreciation of difference, not tolerance of it.

Another large study found age diversity lifted productivity if and only if the work was creative rather than routine. In routine work it can hurt.

Every one of those conditions is cultural. None is a process.

You can appoint an experienced NED tomorrow. That's governance. Whether anyone listens to them — and whether they listen back — is culture, and it never appears in the board minutes.

Gallup's meta-analysis spans 183,806 business units in 90 countries. Top-quartile engagement versus bottom: 23% higher profitability, 78% lower absenteeism, 21% lower turnover. About 70% of the variance traces to the manager.

Process is what stops a business failing. Culture is what makes it win.

03 Six tax timing points UK SME owners are getting wrong in 2026 Most tax planning for an owner-managed business isn't structuring. It's sequencing — and several of the dates moved this year.

Posted 6 September 2026 · Post

Most tax planning for an owner-managed business isn't structuring. It's sequencing — and several of the dates moved this year.

The six

01

Full expensing isn't automatically the right claim

100% relief looks obviously better than 14%. But dispose of an asset you've fully expensed and the whole disposal value returns as a balancing charge (CAA 2001 s59A) — a standalone taxable amount, not netted off the pool. For kit you'll sell on in a few years while it still holds value, AIA or writing-down allowances can leave you better off. The main pool WDA also fell from 18% to 14% on 1 April 2026.

02

ERIS isn't automatically better than the merged R&D scheme

ERIS is worth up to ~27p per £1 against the merged scheme's 14.7–16.2p. But it's only for loss-making SMEs at 30%+ R&D intensity, and that 27p assumes losses to surrender. Near break-even there's little to surrender and the flat merged rate can win.

Harder still: first-time claimants, or anyone who hasn't claimed in three years, must notify HMRC within six months of the period end. Miss it and the claim is gone.

03

An overdrawn director's loan carries two separate charges

s455 rose to 35.75% for loans outstanding from 6 April 2026. Repaying £5k+ then redrawing within 30 days is disregarded, and the refund doesn't arrive until nine months after the end of the period in which you repaid.

Separately: if the balance tops £10,000 at any point and you charge below HMRC's official rate (3.75%), that's a benefit in kind — income tax for you, Class 1A at 15% for the company. Charging interest fixes the second charge and does nothing for the first.

04

Two deductions depend on cash actually moving

Accrued remuneration is only deductible in the period accrued if paid within nine months of the year end (CTA 2009 s1288) — catching holiday pay accruals, not just director bonuses.

Employer pension contributions are deductible only when paid in cleared funds (FA 2004 s196). An accrual won't do.

05

Count your associated companies

The £50k/£250k corporation tax thresholds, the £1.5m instalment threshold and the £5m loss deductions allowance are all divided by associated companies plus one. The marginal rate between £50k and £250k is 26.5% — above the 25% headline — so deductible spend is worth more inside the band than above it.

06

Exit and succession got more expensive — but less than widely reported

BADR is 18% from 6 April 2026 on a £1m lifetime limit. BPR is now capped — but at £2.5m of combined business and agricultural property, not the £1m still being quoted. That was the original 2024 proposal, raised in December 2025 and now law. It's transferable between spouses, so £5m for a couple, with 50% relief above.

The point

None of this is exotic. It's the same decisions you were making anyway, with real cash attached to when you make them.

04 Kodak buried its best idea. Bumble might be next. Two companies. Same mistake, thirty years apart. Rework your strategy to avoid the same.

Posted 5 September 2026 · Post

Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975, then buried it, because film paid the bills. By 1996 it had two-thirds of the global photography market and $16bn in revenue. By 2012 it was bankrupt. The thing that made Kodak great — controlling how, when and how few pictures you took — was exactly what digital photography destroyed.

Bumble, the online dating app, is doing something similar, in real time. Its one distinctive rule, women message first, solved a genuine problem: women drowning in messages, men getting almost none. It took the company to a $14bn IPO in 2021. But paying users and revenue are now both falling, the share price is down roughly 95% since listing, and Bumble has quietly let men message first and stretched its reply window from 24 hours to 72 — dismantling the one thing that made it different from Tinder or Hinge.

  Kodak 1975 – 2012 Bumble 2014 – today
The distinctive thing Control of how, when and how few pictures you took. Women message first.
What it was worth Two-thirds of the global photography market and $16bn revenue by 1996. A $14bn IPO in 2021.
What happened to it Protected until digital photography removed it entirely. Bankrupt by 2012. Being dismantled by choice — men may now message first, and the reply window has gone from 24 hours to 72.
Where that leaves them The market moved and the USP became worthless. Paying users and revenue falling, share price down roughly 95% since listing.

Where the two stories meet

We used to meet someone, then take a photo of the moment. Dating apps and camera phones flipped that order entirely: now you see the photo first, swipe, message — and the actual meeting comes last, if it comes at all.

And the generation that grew up entirely inside that flipped order is now rejecting it on both fronts. Gen Z is driving a genuine boom in film and disposable cameras — Kodak itself just launched a new one — precisely because it's slower, scarcer and more deliberate than a camera roll. The same generation is telling researchers they're burnt out on swiping and want fewer, more intentional matches, and real dates, not more messages.

Kodak protected its USP until the market removed it entirely. Bumble is dismantling its own USP just as the market seems to be asking for exactly what it originally offered.

Same lesson, opposite directions

Know precisely what makes you different, and keep testing — deliberately, regularly — whether the market still wants it. It changes more often than most strategies assume.

05 Why I'm ditching the Chief Finance Officer title The mindset that creates a problem is rarely the one that solves it. Perhaps the C stands for something else.

Posted 14 July 2026 · Post

We often think of a CFO as the Chief Finance Officer. And rightly so — finance is the foundation. It brings discipline, insight and clarity to decision-making. But in today's world, where challenges are more complex and interconnected, maybe the role also needs to be a Chief Facilitator Officer.

The mindset that creates a problem is rarely the one that solves it.

The best CFOs don't just interpret the numbers. They create the environment where better decisions happen. They bring people together across the business. They challenge thinking. They ask the questions others haven't considered. They turn data into dialogue — and dialogue into action.

And in the boardroom

Refreshing mindsets at that level isn't the responsibility of one individual — it's a collective responsibility. But CFOs are uniquely positioned to influence it. Sitting at the intersection of financial insight and commercial reality, they can introduce new perspectives, challenge assumptions, and help the board regularly step back and rethink its approach.

And in many cases a fractional CFO can amplify this further — bringing fresh, external perspective and constructive challenge at relatively low cost, but with disproportionately high influence.

Without that renewal, thinking becomes fixed. Decisions become stale. And opportunities are missed — not because they weren't there, but because the organisation wasn't ready to see them.

The distinction

Finance provides the what and the why. Facilitation unlocks the how. That is often the difference between a good CFO and an exceptional one.

In a world that demands better problem-solving, perhaps the true value of a CFO isn't just in controlling performance — but in enabling it.

Facilitation is becoming a core differentiating leadership skill for CFOs.

06 Do you have a Four I's on your Board? A friend called the fractional CFO a buzz-word for a part-time CFO you hire when you can't afford a real one. I beg to differ.

Posted 6 July 2026 · Post

Last week a friend said to me that the fractional CFO role is just a buzz-word for a part-time CFO, when you can't afford a full-time one. I beg to differ.

Growing companies often think they need a full-time CFO. In reality, what they need is someone who can deliver the Four I's — and a fractional CFO can often deliver them with more intensity and impact. A fractional CFO gives you senior-level impact without senior-level overhead, exactly when you need it most.

In today's environment, many businesses don't need a full-time CFO — they need a high-impact one. That is where a fractional CFO delivers outsized value, bringing the Four I's with a level of focus and pace that is often hard to achieve in a permanent role.

The Four I's

Inspire

A fractional CFO energises teams quickly, aligning people behind a common goal and driving momentum at speed. With no legacy constraints or internal politics, they can cut through noise and create clarity fast.

Influence

They bring an independent, unfiltered voice to the Board. Because they are not embedded in the day-to-day hierarchy, they can challenge assumptions, pressure-test strategy and provide cohesive, commercially grounded guidance that shifts thinking.

Inform

Fractional CFOs are obsessive about decision-quality data. They build reporting that gives leaders an accurate, real-time view of performance and operational health — not just month-end numbers. The result: better decisions, made earlier.

Initiate

They get things done. On time. On budget. In line with strategic goals.

A fractional CFO is often brought in to deliver transformation, stabilise performance or accelerate growth — and they move with urgency because impact is their mandate.

Where the line falls

A full-time CFO is invaluable when scale, complexity and continuity demand it. But for many organisations, a fractional CFO delivers sharper challenge, faster progress and a more concentrated dose of the Four I's — exactly when it matters most.

07 Building a house of sand A room full of trainee accountants had never seen a T-account. Thirty minutes later, the question was what else we have stopped teaching.

Published 17 May 2026 · Article

I ran a Commercial Finance seminar recently for accounting students preparing for their professional exams. While troubleshooting GL posting errors, I reverted to T-accounts to show what was actually happening. One student asked, “What are T-accounts, I've never seen those before?” — a reminder that many young people today have never drawn up accounts by hand. I spent the next 30 minutes teaching the fundamentals of double entry and how those basics map to modern accounting systems. The look of amazement on their faces was both pleasing and sobering.

Matthew 7 describes a man who builds his house on sand; when the storm comes, it collapses. This well-known metaphor shows up in boardrooms for a reason — it captures flawed assumptions, weak structures, and the risk of collapse under pressure.

Three ways the sand shows up

Forecasts built on hope

They collapse at the first storm — unrealistic business cases, or sales-driven forecasting.

Ambition without structure

Instability by another name — over-stretching, M&A integration, or rapid expansion.

Workarounds are sand

Processes are concrete. It is the case for a new ERP, an operational redesign, or a risk review.

The question

If senior leaders understand these lessons, why aren't we embedding them in the teaching of our future finance leaders?

We live in an era of growing AI dependency. That makes it more important, not less, to teach people how to think, see what's happening, and understand why it's happening. Solid foundations matter.

If you benefited from mentors early in your career — and I am sure we all did — pay that forward. Teach, coach and guide wherever you can. We may be leading now, but they will be running the show soon.

08 The child mentality No debt, ideal weight, eight hours' sleep, no stress. This was my peak. I was seven.

Published 14 May 2026 · Article

A shout out to the funniest post I have seen on LinkedIn for a while goes to Kay Wenham, a recruiter in education, whom I have never had the pleasure to meet in person but who knows how to put a smile on other people's faces. She posted the following update:

No debt.
Ideal weight.
8+ hours of sleep.
Mental health on track.
Right nutrition.
Zero alcohol.
No stress.
This was my peak. I was seven.

I chuckled aloud when I read it, and it is something I am sure most of us can relate to.

When I read through the list again, I started to think how many of these applied at key stages in my life, and I saw a trend. The issues around finance and remuneration, and around physical and emotional well-being, always had an inverse relationship with job satisfaction. Enjoy your job, feel appreciated, feel you have achieved — however hard the work may be — and the issues fade away.

How you feel at work is not solely the domain of your manager. You have the biggest influence on how you feel at work. If you don't like how you feel, do something to improve it.

What this taught me

Don't waste your energy tracking the wrong indicators. Forget the reactive stresses above and concentrate on the only proactive indicator that really matters — your happiness level at work. That one will sort out all the others.

Do what makes you happy and every day becomes a joy. In essence, embrace the seven-year-old you.

09 KISS KISS — how a toddler taught me more about leadership clarity than most job descriptions “Work independently and asynchronously to meet deadlines while developing evaluation rubrics and benchmarks.” Meaningless gobbledegook.

Published 12 May 2026 · Article

I was sitting in my home office looking at some job vacancies that had popped up on my feed recently — now deleted — reading the sometimes ridiculous language used in their descriptions. Why would anyone choose to include as a key requirement:

“Work independently and asynchronously to meet deadlines while developing evaluation rubrics and benchmarks to improve critical business model performance.”

Meaningless gobbledegook.

At that exact moment, my young granddaughter walked into the room and said, “Grandad, kiss kiss.” Never has there been more appropriate timing.

We've all heard of the principle Keep It Simple, Stupid — and it feels especially relevant for recruitment jargon. Clear, simple language keeps me engaged. Needing to decipher unnecessary and confusing text just turns me off. LinkedIn contributors beware.

Many authors borrow the acronym KISS, and it's interesting to see how it has evolved to explain different principles and trends. A quick internet search now brings up Keep It Simple, the series of books; Keep It Smart & Sustainable; Keep It Strategic & Simple; Keep Ideas Simple & Scalable — and the list goes on.

That got me thinking about how KISS aligns with my own approach as a fractional CFO — particularly my Four I's methodology: Inspire, Influence, Inform, Initiate.

How KISS connects to the Four I's

The four I's KISS What it buys you
InspireKnow what mattersClarity inspires confidence and alignment.
InfluenceInterpret with insightInsight strengthens challenge and strategic guidance.
InformSimplifyBetter information, simpler decisions.
InitiateStart or stopExecution becomes focused, fast and value-driven.

What's become apparent to me is that most good management principles share similar underlying values. The real difference between success and failure isn't the label or the framework — it's how you deliver it.

A clear objective and razor-sharp focus on delivery, underpinned by hard work, determination and teamwork, will always create a greater chance of success.

But for now, I think I'll go and get that kiss from my granddaughter.
